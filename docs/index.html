Which pages should a content reviewer check first?

Susheel Kumar · October 2026 · Decision-support research on 30,000 starter pages + a forward test on 113,588 warehouse pages

Abstract

FlyRank flags pages for refresh or click-through fixes with hand-written rules, but a content reviewer can only open a few dozen pages a week, so this study asks which pages they should open first. I built and audited the method on 30,000 pseudonymised pages from 32 client sites in the FlyRank ML Internship release, then repeated it on the full warehouse: 113,588 pages from 33 clients, one month later than the model was trained on. I tested the signals behind two of FlyRank's flags, turned the one that held up into a transparent rule, and compared it with learned rankers scored only on clients they never saw and only on data from before the outcome window. On the starter data the final ranker put 86% declining pages in its top 50 (rule 70%, random 54%), and on the warehouse forward test it held 68–71% across the top 100–1,000 pages against 33% at random, while the rule fell to or below random beyond about the top 100 pages and gradient boosting stayed below random throughout. The output is a capped, human-reviewed queue for review order — decision-support, not a forecast, and not evidence that any action prevents decline.

Key findings

  • One flag signal held, one didn't. Low CTR for a page's position goes with decline inside 15 of 16 clients. "Stale" pages only look riskier because of which clients update in batches.
  • A simple model beats the rule: P@50 0.86 vs 0.70, with a base rate of 0.54.
  • Honest validation changed the winner. Gradient boosting's lead came from inputs that overlap the outcome window.
  • It held up forward in time on the full warehouse: 0.68–0.71 of the top 100–1,000 pages declined, vs 0.33 at random. The rule and gradient boosting did not hold up.
  • It finds decline, not value. The top 50 pages had only 10 clicks between them.

1The content problem this comes from

A typical week: a reviewer looks after hundreds of published articles across several client sites. Some are slipping in search — fewer people see them each week — but most teams notice only after the traffic is gone. There is time to open perhaps fifty pages. Which fifty?

FlyRank already answers this with rules. Its product flags pages that look stale, pages whose click-through is weak for their position, and "quick wins". FlyRank's own March 2026 research report recommends the same priorities in prose: refresh mature pages before they decay, and improve click capture on pages that already rank. Rules like these are readable and they run in production — but their thresholds are chosen by hand, and nobody had checked how well they order a review list on held-out clients.

This paper is that check, done as a case study on FlyRank's own data. It asks three things:

  1. Do the signals behind the flags hold up? Staleness (behind the refresh flags) and CTR-versus-position (behind the CTR-fix flag).
  2. Can a simple learned ranker order the review list better than a rule built from those signals?
  3. How much of any gain survives honest validation — new clients, and no peeking at the period the outcome is measured in?

The short answers: one signal held and one did not; yes, a plain logistic regression beats the rule; and less survives than a standard workflow would report, which changed which model I would recommend.

2Data

  • Source: the FlyRank ML Internship starter release — Google Search Console and Google Analytics 4 metrics, pseudonymised before release. There are no client names, domains, URLs, page titles or search queries.
  • Size: 30,000 pages from 32 client sites. Each page has one 90-day performance window plus two 30-day comparison windows.
  • Population: every page is at least 90 days old and had at least one impression in the window.
  • Outcome (used only to grade rankings): a page counts as declining when its impressions fell more than 20% between the last 30 days and the 30 days before. That covers 16,262 pages (54.2%), so random ordering scores 0.54 at any list length.
  • Never used as inputs: the outcome fields themselves and the two 30-day windows that build them. Site IDs were used only to group the validation, never as features. The release contains no product flags or health scores.
  • Warehouse release: from the full release (about 79 million daily rows), I used four monthly partitions of the daily performance table (December 2025 to March 2026) plus the content and client tables. These are mid-panel months; the sealed final month (June 2026) was not touched.
    • Only rows where Search Console data actually exists were kept (gsc_data_available IS TRUE).
    • Only clients whose Search Console history starts by 1 December 2025 were kept: 39 of 104.
    • Only pages created before their feature month were kept.
    That leaves 103,158 pages from 35 clients for training and 113,588 pages from 33 clients for testing.

3Methodology

Step 1 — Check the signals before trusting them

  • Staleness: mixed. Pooled across all pages, pages last updated 91–120 days ago declined more (61% vs 51%). But updates arrive in client-wide batches — two clients hold two-thirds of that bucket — and inside individual clients the gap was about zero. The common "180 days stale" threshold fired on only 17 pages with real traffic.
  • CTR versus position: confirmed, for positions 1–20. The further a page's click-through falls below the median for its position band, the more often it declined. This held inside 15 of the 16 clients large enough to check.

Step 2 — A transparent baseline

A page is flagged if it ranks in positions 1–20, has at least 500 impressions, and its CTR is less than half the median for its position band. Flagged pages are ranked by how far CTR falls short, scaled by the log of impressions. The rule has one reason code, one action ("rewrite title/meta") and no fitted weights.

Step 3 — Learned rankers

I compared logistic regression, a depth-3 decision tree, random forest and gradient boosting. Each model's predicted probability is used as a ranking and graded with precision@K: of the top K pages, the share that actually declined.

Step 4 — Validation designed to be hard to fool

  • Grouped by client. Five folds, so every page is scored by a model that never saw any page from its site. This mirrors adding a new client.
  • Time-aware inputs. The outcome compares days 1–30 with days 31–60 before export, but the standard 90-day totals include those days. I rebuilt traffic for days 61–90 exactly (90-day total minus the two 30-day windows; never negative). The final model uses only that earlier window plus page metadata.
  • Leakage checks. I planted the outcome field as a feature: accuracy jumped to near-perfect (AUC 1.00), which shows the test harness catches leaks. I also ran drop-one tests, a feature timeline, and a scan for product flags.

What the final model leans on is unsurprising. Its three largest weights are:

  • Earlier impressions — push toward decline.
  • Earlier clicks — push away from decline.
  • Page age — younger pages lean toward decline.

Together, the first two are a smoother version of the CTR signal behind FlyRank's CTR-fix flag: pages that many people saw but few clicked. No single input is suspiciously strong; the best single feature alone reaches an AUC of only about 0.59.

The forward test on the full warehouse

The starter results come from one snapshot. To check that the method also works later in time and on new clients, I re-ran it on the warehouse with a strict design:

  • Training: December features predict whether February impressions fell more than 20% below January.
  • Testing: January features predict whether March fell below February.
  • Unseen clients: every test client was held out of training (5 client-grouped folds).
  • Clean inputs: features always come from the month before the outcome window.

How to read it:

  • The final ranker generalises where it matters — the top of the review list.
  • With the per-site cap, its top 50 held 35 declining pages (0.70) across 14 clients.
  • Its whole-list ordering is weak. Its AUC is 0.58, and 0.49 once pages with fewer than 20 base-month impressions are removed. Its value is in picking a short list, not in ranking every page.
  • Gradient boosting failing again, now forward in time, confirms the Week-6 decision to ship the simpler model.
  • An earlier warehouse model fell below its base rate. It used outcome-month features and a random split, and scored 0.793 accuracy against a 0.812 base rate. That lesson shaped this design.

5Limitations and honest framing

Intended use: deciding which pages a human reviewer opens first.

Not intended for:

  • automatic edits;
  • forecasting next month's traffic;
  • judging writers or vendors;
  • telling a client their content "is declining".

How it was evaluated: every page was scored by a model that never saw its client, using inputs from before the outcome window, and graded by precision@K against the base rate.

The biggest one: the model finds decline, not value. Its 50 most confident picks had only 10 clicks between them in the earlier window. A list weighted by clicks covered 21,167 clicks, but only 48% of those pages declined — below random. Losing impressions on a page nobody clicks costs little.
  • One snapshot, same-period outcome. The outcome compares two recent 30-day windows. This ranks pages for review; it does not forecast next month.
  • No causal claim. No page here was randomly refreshed or rewritten, so nothing shows that an action prevents decline. The same applies to "refreshed vs not" comparisons: in this slice they are mostly comparisons between client sites.
  • Client effects are large. 7 of 32 sites had a decline rate below 20% or above 80%, where page-level ranking adds little. In raw model order, one site filled 84% of the top 200.
  • Whole-list ranking is weak on the warehouse. AUC is 0.58, and 0.49 on pages with at least 20 base-month impressions. Use the top of the list, not the full order.
  • Outcome noise. 15% of "declines" rest on fewer than 20 earlier impressions, for example a single impression falling to zero.
  • One pattern dominates the top. 82% of the top 50 had zero earlier clicks despite hundreds of impressions. Some of these may be tracking gaps rather than real pages in trouble.
  • Scope. The warehouse forward test covers one train/test pair of months (December→February and January→March). Only 39 of 104 clients had enough history to include, and only pages that existed and were visible in the base month were scored. More months, and the newest clients, are untested.

6Ranked recommendations

  1. Run two short lists, not one. A risk queue of the top 50 by score, capped at 10 pages per site (the cap costs no precision: 0.86 vs 0.84 uncapped). Plus a value lane of the top 20 by score × earlier clicks.
  2. Check tracking before editing any page with zero clicks on 300+ impressions.
  3. Skip pages updated in the last 60 days (69% of the top 200). Wait for the last change to show up before acting again.
  4. Treat many queue pages from one site as one case. Look for a site-level cause first: a migration, a tracking change, or an algorithm update.
  5. Keep humans on every edit. Do not automate rewrites, merges, redirects, deletions, or messages to clients that their content "is declining".
  6. Next step: a click-weighted outcome, so risk and value point at the same pages. On the warehouse, the capped top 50 covered only 50 feature-month clicks, and 76% of those pages had zero clicks. Then repeat the forward test across more month pairs.

Each page in the exported queue carries an action, a reason code (for example low_ctr_page_1_2 | earlier_ctr_low) and three review flags. The capped top 50 found 43 declining pages in about 12.5 review hours, versus 30 for a random 50.
